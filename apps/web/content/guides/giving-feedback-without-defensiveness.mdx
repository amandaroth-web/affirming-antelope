To give feedback without making employees defensive, ask permission first, describe one specific behavior, and ask how it looks from their side before you share your view. A pattern from motivational interviewing called **elicit, provide, elicit** helps here. When the standard isn't negotiable, say so once and plainly, then let the employee decide how they'll meet it.

## The two threats in feedback

Feedback threatens two things people care about: their sense of competence and their sense of control. A label ("You're careless") attacks competence. A lecture with a ready-made fix ("Here's what you need to do") takes away control. When both happen at once, the employee spends the rest of the meeting protecting themselves, and none of your feedback lands.

MI offers ways to lower both threats without watering down what you need to say.

## Ask permission

Asking permission before giving feedback sounds soft, but it works even when the feedback isn't optional. It gives the person a moment to get ready, and it tells them you respect their right to be part of the conversation.

<Example speaker="Clinic manager" kind="avoid">Hey, I need to talk to you about how you handled that patient.</Example>
<Example speaker="Clinic manager">I have some feedback about the conversation with Mr. Haddad this morning. Is now okay, or would after clinic be better?</Example>

Be honest about what the permission covers: when you talk, and maybe how. If you ask "Is it okay if I give you some feedback?" and they say no, you'll need to explain that this one is required, which is worse than being clear up front.

## Describe one behavior

Specific feedback about one behavior is easier to hear than a general judgment. It also gives the employee something they can change.

<Example speaker="Physical therapy manager" kind="avoid">You've been pretty careless with your notes lately.</Example>
<Example speaker="Physical therapy manager">Four of your notes this week were missing the functional goals section. The insurer flagged two of them.</Example>

"Careless" invites a debate about whether they're careless. "Four notes, missing the goals section" is a fact you can both look at. If you can't name the specific behavior, you're not ready to give the feedback.

## Elicit, provide, elicit

Elicit, provide, elicit (EPE), sometimes called ask, offer, ask, is MI's main tool for sharing information. Managers can use it for nearly any piece of corrective feedback.

1. **Elicit.** Ask what they already know, or how it looks from their side.
2. **Provide.** Share your information or observation in a sentence or two.
3. **Elicit.** Ask what they make of it.

A short conversation shows the full pattern.

<Example speaker="Physical therapy manager" kind="open-question">How have the notes been going since we switched templates?</Example>
<Example speaker="Physical therapist">Fine, mostly. The new one's longer.</Example>
<Example speaker="Physical therapy manager">Four of your notes this week were missing the functional goals section, and the insurer flagged two. Without that section, they can deny the visit.</Example>
<Example speaker="Physical therapy manager" kind="open-question">What do you make of that?</Example>
<Example speaker="Physical therapist">Oh. I didn't know they were denying for it. The goals are on the second tab, and I think I've been skipping it when I'm behind.</Example>

The first elicit told you what the therapist knew. The provide step gave him the missing piece, the insurer's rule, in two sentences. The final question let him connect the dots himself, and he named the cause: a skipped tab on busy days. That's more useful than anything you'd have guessed, and he's more likely to fix a cause he found.

Keep the provide step short. If you talk for two minutes, it becomes a lecture, and the second elicit will get a shrug.

## Hold the standard and support autonomy

Some feedback comes with a fixed expectation. MI doesn't ask you to hide it. The skill is stating the standard once, in clear words, and then giving the employee real control over the how.

<Example speaker="Physical therapist">So what do you want me to do?</Example>
<Example speaker="Physical therapy manager" kind="avoid">Well, ideally it would be great if the goals were in there, if you can manage it.</Example>
<Example speaker="Physical therapy manager">Every note needs the goals section completed before you sign. How you make that happen on a busy day is up to you. What would work?</Example>

The vague version makes the standard sound optional. The clear one is direct about the standard and hands the method back. Employees tend to respond better to a clear requirement with autonomy than to a soft request with a hidden requirement behind it.

When an employee asks to be told what to do, respect that.

## If they get defensive anyway

Even good feedback can sting. When the employee pushes back, the urge is to make your case again, louder. Reflect instead.

<Example speaker="Respiratory therapist">So now I'm getting written up for being two minutes late to a code? I was in another room.</Example>
<Example speaker="RT supervisor" kind="avoid">Nobody's writing you up. But we have response time standards for a reason.</Example>
<Example speaker="RT supervisor" kind="complex-reflection">You were with another patient, and this feels like being blamed for something you couldn't help.</Example>
<Example speaker="Respiratory therapist">Yeah. I'd have been there faster if I could.</Example>
<Example speaker="RT supervisor">I believe you, and you're not being written up. I'd like to look at how the pages get routed, because the response time standard still matters. Can we figure that out together?</Example>

The reflection lowered the temperature, and the therapist said something you can agree with. Then the supervisor stated the standard and turned the conversation toward a shared problem. ## Feedback to a peer or a more senior colleague

Charge nurses, leads and new managers often give feedback to people with more tenure than they have. Permission matters even more here, and so does being specific about your role.

<Example speaker="Lead MA">Can I mention something about the vaccine fridge log? I know you've been doing this longer than me.</Example>
<Example speaker="Senior MA">Go ahead.</Example>
<Example speaker="Lead MA">The afternoon temperature wasn't logged three days last week. The vaccine program audits those logs. How have afternoons been for you?</Example>

The lead acknowledged the seniority without apologizing for raising it. Then she gave the fact and the reason, and asked for the senior MA's view.

## Positive feedback needs the same care

Generic praise ("Great job!") is easy to give and easy to forget. Specific feedback on what went well tells the employee what to keep doing. MI calls this an **affirmation**: naming a particular effort or strength you saw.

<Example speaker="Clinic manager" kind="affirmation">When Mr. Haddad got upset about the wait, you sat down at his eye level and explained the delay. He calmed down within a minute.</Example>

Give positive feedback on its own, at a different time from corrective feedback. If you always pair them, as in the feedback sandwich, employees learn that praise is a warning, and they stop trusting it.

## Feedback in a few sentences

Not every piece of feedback needs a meeting. On a busy unit or clinic floor, a ten-second version of EPE works.

<Example speaker="Charge nurse">Quick thing on bed 6. Did you see the second identifier on that blood draw?</Example>
<Example speaker="Staff nurse">I checked the wristband.</Example>
<Example speaker="Charge nurse">Our policy is name and date of birth out loud, plus the band. Can you do that on the next one?</Example>

The question invites them in, and the information takes one sentence.

## The limits of MI for feedback

- **Safety in the moment.** If a patient or coworker is at risk right now, give direction first: "Stop, check the line before you push that." Feedback and reflection can come afterward.
- **Policy violations and misconduct.** Harassment and drug diversion call for a formal process. Describe what happened, follow HR guidance, and don't use open questions to coax a confession.
- **When you've already decided.** If a consequence is set, say so. Using EPE to steer someone to a conclusion you've already reached is manipulation, and they'll feel it.
