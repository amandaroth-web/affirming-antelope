Motivational interviewing (MI) is a collaborative way of talking with someone about change. Instead of supplying the reasons yourself, you help the person find and say their own, and then you plan with them. William Miller and Stephen Rollnick developed it in addiction treatment in the 1980s, and clinicians, coaches and managers now use it wherever a conversation about change keeps stalling.

## A working definition

Miller and Rollnick have refined their definition in each edition of their book, *Motivational Interviewing*. Paraphrased, MI is a way of having a conversation about change that strengthens a person's own motivation and commitment, carried out in an atmosphere of acceptance and compassion.

Two parts of that sentence do most of the work.

- **It has a direction.** MI is about a specific change: taking a medication, cutting back on drinking, getting reports in on time. Warm listening with no direction is good counseling, but it isn't MI.
- **The motivation belongs to them.** You aren't installing reasons. You're helping someone hear the reasons they already have, often for the first time out loud.

## Why telling people to change often backfires

Most people who need to make a change already feel two ways about it. They want to sleep better and they want the late-night scrolling. They know the deadline matters and they resent how it was set. MI calls this **ambivalence**, and it treats ambivalence as normal.

When you argue for change, you take one side of that ambivalence. The other person, almost by reflex, voices the other side. Then they've spent the conversation making the case against change, and people tend to believe what they hear themselves say.

The patient's second answer is **change talk**: their own words in favor of change. MI is built around hearing that kind of talk and helping the person say more of it. You can learn to spot it in [change talk vs sustain talk](/guides/change-talk-vs-sustain-talk).

The urge to fix, correct and advise has a name in MI: the **righting reflex**. It comes from caring, and it's the habit most learners spend the longest unlearning.

## The spirit of MI

Miller and Rollnick insist that MI is a mindset first and a set of techniques second. You can ask perfect open questions and still sound like an interrogation. In the fourth edition they describe the spirit with four elements.

### Partnership

You work with the person, not on them. You bring expertise, and they bring expertise about their own life, body or job. A nurse might say, "You know your routine better than I do. Let's figure out what could fit."

### Acceptance

Acceptance has four parts: respecting the person's worth, trying to understand their view (accurate empathy), honoring their right to choose (autonomy), and noticing their strengths (affirmation). Acceptance doesn't mean approval. A manager can accept that someone is frustrated with a policy and still expect them to follow it.

### Compassion

You keep the other person's wellbeing at the center. This is what separates MI from sales technique. If the change you're steering toward mainly serves you, the conversation isn't MI.

### Empowerment

You help people notice their own strengths and their ability to change, so they leave the conversation more capable. Earlier editions called this element **evocation**, meaning you draw out the person's own ideas rather than installing yours. You'll see both terms, and the app's lessons still use evocation. The idea underneath is the same: assume they already have reasons and resources, and help them find those.

## The four processes

MI conversations move through four overlapping processes. They build on each other, but real conversations loop back when something slips.

1. **Engaging.** Building enough trust that a real conversation can happen. A pharmacist asks, "Before we get into the refill, how have things been with the new medication?"
2. **Focusing.** Agreeing on what you'll talk about. A clinic manager asks permission: "Would it be okay if we spent a few minutes on the chart notes?"
3. **Evoking.** Drawing out the person's own reasons for change. "What would be better for you if the notes were done before you left?"
4. **Planning.** Helping them turn readiness into a specific next step, in their words. "What's one thing you'd want to try this week?"

A common mistake is skipping straight to planning. If a patient hasn't voiced any reason to change, a detailed plan will sit unused. If a conversation stalls, check whether engagement or focus has slipped before you push harder.

## The core skills

The everyday tools of MI are known as **OARS**:

- **Open questions** that invite a story instead of a yes or no.
- **Affirmations** that name a specific strength or effort.
- **Reflections** that say back what you heard, sometimes with added meaning.
- **Summaries** that gather several things the person has said.

Two more skills come up often: **informing and advising with permission**, and **recognizing and responding to change talk**. MI doesn't forbid giving advice. It asks you to offer it when the person wants it and to leave their choice intact.

## Common misunderstandings

**"MI means never telling anyone anything."** You can share information, express concern and state requirements. MI shapes how and when you do it, usually by asking first what the person knows and whether they'd like to hear more.

**"MI is the stages of change model."** The transtheoretical model (precontemplation, contemplation and so on) grew up alongside MI and the two are often taught together, but they're separate ideas. You can practice MI without staging anyone.

**"MI is a way to get compliance."** If you use it to corner someone, they feel it, and the conversation turns into a contest.

**"Being nice is enough."** Warmth matters, but MI also takes technical skill: noticing change talk, choosing what to reflect, and timing a shift toward planning.

## What the evidence says, in plain terms

MI has a substantial research base, with many controlled trials across healthcare settings. The strongest and most studied areas include alcohol and other substance use, and there's a large body of work on health behaviors such as smoking, physical activity, diet and taking medication.

Three honest caveats:

- **Effects vary.** Across studies, MI tends to help on average, but results differ widely by setting, problem and practitioner. It is not a guaranteed fix.
- **Skill matters.** Studies that measure how well clinicians deliver MI tend to find that skillful MI works better than MI in name only. A workshop alone rarely produces lasting skill. Ongoing practice with feedback is what moves people.
- **Leadership evidence is thin.** Most of the research comes from healthcare and addiction treatment. Using MI in management is a reasonable adaptation of the same principles, but it hasn't been studied in nearly the same depth.

If you want the primary source, read Miller and Rollnick's *Motivational Interviewing: Helping People Change and Grow* (4th edition).

## Where MI fits

### In healthcare

MI earns its place in conversations where the person is unsure and the choice is theirs to make: chronic disease self-management, alcohol and drug use, smoking, medication adherence, weight, physical activity, and follow-through on referrals. It fits brief visits too. A few well-placed reflections and one evocative question can change the direction of a ten-minute appointment. See [MI for clinicians](/for/clinicians) for how the app's practice fits clinical work.

### In leadership

Managers use MI for missed deadlines, performance conversations, burnout check-ins and resistance to change. The manager's role adds complications, because you hold power and you have a stake in the outcome. The [MI for managers guide](/guides/motivational-interviewing-for-managers) covers how to handle that openly.

### When MI isn't the right tool

- **Emergencies and safety issues.** If someone is in danger, act and direct.
- **When the person is already ready.** If they've decided and want a plan, help them plan. Evoking more motivation wastes their time.
- **When you need to make the decision.** Some calls are yours. Say so plainly rather than dressing a directive up as a choice.
- **When there's no ambivalence to work with.** If someone sees no problem and you have no legitimate concern to raise, there may be nothing to talk about yet.

## Where to start

MI is simple to describe and hard to do when the conversation gets tense. The habits that make it work, like reflecting before you advise, only stick with repetition. Start small: one skill, short practice sessions, and honest feedback.
