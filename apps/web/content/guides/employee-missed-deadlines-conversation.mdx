To talk to an employee about missed deadlines, name the issue early with specifics, then ask what's been getting in the way before you offer any fixes. Be clear that the deadline stands, and let the employee work out how they'll meet it. Repeated lateness usually has a specific cause, and the employee is often the person best placed to find it.

## Before the conversation

Get the facts straight: which deadlines slipped, and what the delay affected. "Late a lot" invites an argument about the word "a lot." "The credentialing files for the three new providers were each a week past the payer deadline, and one start date moved" gives you both something concrete to talk about.

Decide what's fixed and what's open. In most cases the deadline is fixed and the method is open. Sometimes the deadline itself is negotiable, and if so, you should know that before you walk in. Going in clear about both makes it easier to be direct about one and curious about the other.

## Name it in the first minute

An employee who has been missing deadlines usually knows why you want to talk. Dancing around it for ten minutes makes them more anxious, and it can feel like a setup when you finally get there.

<Example speaker="Credentialing specialist">So I'm guessing this is about the files.</Example>
<Example speaker="Medical staff office manager" kind="avoid">Oh, no, I just wanted to check in generally. How's everything going?</Example>
<Example speaker="Medical staff office manager">Yes, it is. The last three files went in a week late, and Dr. Nwosu's start date had to move. I'm not here to write you up. I'd like to understand what's been happening.</Example>

The better version confirms the topic with specifics and lowers the stakes in a few sentences. "I'm not here to write you up" only works if it's true, so leave it out if a formal process is already underway.

## Get curious about the cause

Your first theory about why someone is late is often wrong. Open questions get you the real story, and a reflection after each answer keeps it coming.

<Example speaker="Medical staff office manager" kind="open-question">What's been getting in the way with the files?</Example>
<Example speaker="Credentialing specialist">The verifications. Half the residency programs don't answer email anymore, so I'm calling and waiting on hold.</Example>
<Example speaker="Medical staff office manager" kind="reflection">You're spending your time chasing other people's answers.</Example>
<Example speaker="Credentialing specialist">Yeah. And I don't start chasing until the packet's complete, which is the other problem. Some providers take weeks to send their stuff.</Example>

Two reflections in, you've learned about a sequencing problem that a lecture on time management would never have surfaced. Some causes of missed deadlines belong to the employee, and some belong to the process or to you. You need to know which before you can fix anything.

### Ask about the person, too

Sometimes the cause isn't the work. A strong performer who starts missing deadlines may be dealing with something at home or a problem with a coworker. An open question leaves room for that without prying.

<Example speaker="Team lead" kind="open-question">You used to be the one who finished early. What's changed for you over the last few months?</Example>

If they don't want to go there, let it go. You've shown you're interested in them as well as the output.

## Respond to sustain talk without arguing

You'll often hear reasons why the deadline doesn't matter, or why it isn't their fault. That's **sustain talk**, and arguing with it tends to make the employee defend it harder.

<Example speaker="Quality coordinator">Honestly, nobody looks at the audit data until the committee meets on the 20th. Submitting it on the 5th is arbitrary.</Example>
<Example speaker="Quality director" kind="avoid">It's not arbitrary. The deadline is the deadline.</Example>
<Example speaker="Quality director" kind="reflection">From where you sit, the 5th looks like a date somebody picked.</Example>
<Example speaker="Quality coordinator">Kind of, yeah.</Example>
<Example speaker="Quality director">Would it help if I explained what happens to it between the 5th and the 20th?</Example>

The reflection takes the heat out. The permission question opens the door to the information the coordinator is missing: two other analysts need the data to build the committee packet. People push back hardest on deadlines they don't understand the reason for.

## The third conversation

If you've had this conversation before, the employee may be tired of it, and you may be too. The temptation is to get firmer and talk more. MI suggests the opposite: emphasize that the choice is theirs, while being honest about the stakes.

<Example speaker="Analyst">Look, I've heard all this. I get the work done.</Example>
<Example speaker="Revenue cycle manager" kind="avoid">Apparently not, or we wouldn't be here again.</Example>
<Example speaker="Revenue cycle manager">You're tired of having this conversation, and I don't blame you. How you get the reports in is up to you. I do need to be straight with you: if they're late again next month, I'll have to start a formal improvement plan. I'd rather we figure it out before that.</Example>

The consequence appears once, in plain words, with no threat in the tone. Then the manager goes back to the employee's choice. MI is compatible with honesty about consequences, and the employee deserves to hear them before any formal meeting.

## Draw out their reasons

The most durable reasons to meet a deadline are the employee's own. Listen for them, and ask for them.

<Example speaker="Team lead" kind="open-question">What would be different for you if the reports were in on time every month?</Example>
<Example speaker="Analyst">I wouldn't have finance emailing me on the 4th. And I'd stop feeling like I'm the problem on the team.</Example>
<Example speaker="Team lead" kind="complex-reflection">You want to be someone the team relies on, and right now it feels like the opposite.</Example>

The analyst named what his lateness costs the team, in his own words, and that carries more weight than hearing it from you. For more on hearing this kind of language, see [how to respond to sustain talk and discord](/guides/responding-to-sustain-talk).

## Let them build the plan

Once the employee is talking about change, ask what they'll do. Their plan, in their words, is the one they'll follow.

<Example speaker="Medical staff office manager" kind="open-question">What would you want to try for the next round of files?</Example>
<Example speaker="Credentialing specialist">I could start verifications as soon as I have the CV. I don't need the full packet for that.</Example>
<Example speaker="Medical staff office manager" kind="open-question">That makes sense. What might get in the way?</Example>

Asking what might get in the way prepares them for the first obstacle. Close with a specific check-in date. "Let's touch base in two weeks, after the Garcia and Lindqvist files are due" works better than "Let me know how it goes."

### When the plan doesn't hold

Sometimes the employee misses the next deadline too. Open the follow-up the same way: ask what happened before you share your disappointment, and affirm any part of the plan that worked. "You started the verifications early on two of the three. What got in the way on the third?" keeps the employee problem-solving with you. If the pattern continues after a fair try, that's when the consequence you named earlier comes into play, and you follow through on it.

## Fix your part

Some missed deadlines come from unclear priorities or a process nobody owns. If you learn that, say so and act on it. If you listen well and then change nothing, your team will stop telling you what's wrong.

## The limits of MI for missed deadlines

- **Deadlines tied to patient safety or regulation.** Critical results reporting and licensure renewals need direction. State the requirement and the timeline plainly.
- **A formal process already underway.** If HR is involved, follow its process. Use reflections to keep the meeting respectful, but don't imply choices that aren't available.
- **When the employee asks to be told.** If someone says "Just tell me what you want," tell them. You can still check how it fits their workflow.
