Good coaching conversations for nurse managers and charge nurses are short and happen often, close to the work. Ask an open question, reflect what you hear, affirm something specific, and ask what the nurse will try next. When a patient is at risk, skip the coaching and give direction plainly, then come back to the conversation once everyone is safe.

## Coaching on a nursing unit

Most coaching advice assumes a quiet office and an hour on the calendar. A med-surg unit at 0700 has neither. Nurse managers coach in the hallway or in the five minutes before a huddle. Charge nurses coach peers they'll work beside for the next twelve hours, often with no formal authority over them.

Motivational interviewing suits these conditions better than you might expect. Its core moves are brief. A good open question takes five seconds, and a reflection takes less. You don't need a full MI conversation to use the skills inside one.

## A five-minute structure

When you have a few minutes, this sequence covers most coaching moments.

1. **Ask.** One open question about how it's going or what happened.
2. **Reflect.** Say back the core of what you heard, including the feeling.
3. **Affirm.** Name one specific thing they did well or a strength they showed.
4. **Evoke.** Ask what they'd want to do next time, or what they think would help.

The sequence leaves your advice out on purpose. You can offer it, with permission, after step four, and often you won't need to.

## Coaching new graduate nurses

New grads tend to judge themselves harshly, and a near miss can feel like proof they don't belong. Reassurance ("Don't be so hard on yourself") sounds kind and doesn't land, because it argues with how they feel. An affirmation works better, because it points to something they did.

<Example speaker="New graduate nurse">I didn't reassess the fall risk after we gave him the Ativan. The night nurse caught it. I keep missing things.</Example>
<Example speaker="Nurse manager" kind="avoid">Hey, you're doing great. Everybody misses stuff their first year.</Example>
<Example speaker="Nurse manager" kind="affirmation">You came and told me yourself, and you already know exactly which step you missed. That's how good nurses get better.</Example>
<Example speaker="New graduate nurse">I guess. I just don't want it to happen again.</Example>
<Example speaker="Nurse manager" kind="open-question">What would help you catch it next time?</Example>

The affirmation names two real behaviors: reporting it and knowing the step. It doesn't tell her how to feel. Then the open question puts her in charge of the fix, which builds the judgment she'll need when you're not around.

Keep the affirmation true. If you affirm everything, new nurses learn to discount it.

## Coaching around breaks and self-care

Nurses who skip breaks often have a patient-centered reason, and arguing with it puts you on the wrong side of their values. A looking-forward question lets them weigh the cost themselves.

<Example speaker="Staff nurse">If I go to lunch, my patients sit with whoever's covering. I'd rather push through.</Example>
<Example speaker="Charge nurse" kind="avoid">You have to take your break. It's policy.</Example>
<Example speaker="Charge nurse" kind="reflection">You don't want your patients to get less because you stepped away.</Example>
<Example speaker="Staff nurse">Right.</Example>
<Example speaker="Charge nurse" kind="open-question">If you keep skipping lunch for the next six months, how do you think hour eleven will look?</Example>

If breaks are required by policy or labor rules in your setting, say that too, once. Then go back to the how: "The break isn't optional. Let's figure out who you'd trust to cover your patients."

## Charge nurses coaching peers

A charge nurse often has responsibility without authority. You're expected to keep the shift safe and the assignments moving, and the people you're coaching may have more years on the unit than you. Permission is the tool that makes this work.

<Example speaker="Charge nurse">Hey, Rosa. Can I mention something from report this morning? It's small.</Example>
<Example speaker="Staff nurse">Sure.</Example>
<Example speaker="Charge nurse">You gave report at the station for bed 14 and skipped the bedside piece. How did that one go from your side?</Example>
<Example speaker="Staff nurse">He was finally asleep. I didn't want to wake him.</Example>
<Example speaker="Charge nurse" kind="reflection">You were protecting his sleep.</Example>
<Example speaker="Staff nurse">Yeah. I know we're supposed to do it bedside, though.</Example>

The charge nurse described what she saw, asked for Rosa's view, and reflected a reasonable motive. Rosa then named the expectation herself. From there, the two of them can talk about doing a quiet bedside check without waking the patient.

### Keep it private and specific

Peer coaching goes badly when it happens in front of others. Correcting a colleague at the station costs them face with the people they work beside. Pull them aside, describe one behavior, and keep your interpretation out of it. "You skipped the bedside handoff" is specific. "You've been cutting corners" is a judgment, and it will start an argument.

## Nurse managers coaching charge nurses

Charge nurses need coaching too, often on the same skills they're trying to use with others. A common sticking point is a charge nurse who takes every hard task because it's faster than asking.

<Example speaker="Charge nurse">If I ask Marcus to take the admission, he'll complain for twenty minutes. It's easier to just take it myself.</Example>
<Example speaker="Nurse manager" kind="complex-reflection">Taking it yourself saves the argument, and it also leaves you with the hardest assignment on most shifts.</Example>
<Example speaker="Charge nurse">Every shift. I'm exhausted by the time I'm supposed to be helping everyone else.</Example>
<Example speaker="Nurse manager" kind="open-question">What would you want to be able to say to Marcus, if the twenty minutes of complaining weren't a factor?</Example>

That question lets her rehearse the conversation she's been avoiding, with you, before she has it on the floor. For more on handling an overloaded lead, see [what to say when an employee says they're overwhelmed](/guides/employee-says-overwhelmed).

## Coaching after a hard shift or a code

Debriefs after a rapid response, a code or a death on the unit are coaching moments too. Staff often replay what they could have done differently, and a manager who jumps straight to the timeline can make them feel investigated. Start with how they're doing, then move to what happened.

<Example speaker="Staff nurse">I keep thinking I should have called the rapid response twenty minutes earlier.</Example>
<Example speaker="Nurse manager" kind="avoid">Well, let's look at the vitals and see when the trigger criteria were met.</Example>
<Example speaker="Nurse manager" kind="complex-reflection">You're carrying that twenty minutes with you.</Example>
<Example speaker="Staff nurse">Yeah. His pressure was soft, but he was talking to me, so I waited.</Example>

You'll still need to review the timeline, and you may find a real practice gap. Hearing the nurse first tells you what she's already concluded, and it keeps her willing to talk in the formal review. If the event has a formal review process, follow it, and be clear with her about what's a supportive conversation and what's part of the review.

## Veteran nurses and change

Experienced nurses carry the unit's memory, including every initiative that came and went. When they push back on a change, reflect the experience before you explain the plan. If you're new to the unit, that matters even more.

<Example speaker="Night charge nurse">You've been here three months. I've been here since before the tower was built.</Example>
<Example speaker="Nurse manager" kind="avoid">I've managed units bigger than this one.</Example>
<Example speaker="Nurse manager" kind="complex-reflection">You know this unit in ways I haven't learned yet, and you want me to respect that.</Example>

Matching credentials turns the conversation into a contest. The reflection hands him the expertise he's defending, which tends to lower his guard.

## The limits of coaching on a nursing unit

Some moments need direction, and nursing has more of them than most fields.

- **A patient at risk right now.** "Stop. Let's double-check that heparin rate together before it runs." Say it plainly. Coach afterward, when the patient is safe.
- **Reportable events and unsafe practice.** Medication errors and impairment have reporting rules in your organization and sometimes outside it. Follow them. MI can shape how you talk with the nurse, but it doesn't replace the process.
- **Standards that aren't up for debate.** Hand hygiene and two-identifier checks are required. State the standard once and use MI for how they'll meet it on a heavy shift.
