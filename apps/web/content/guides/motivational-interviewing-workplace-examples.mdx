These motivational interviewing examples show short manager and employee exchanges from healthcare workplaces, organized by the four OARS skills: open questions, affirmations, reflections and summaries. Each example pairs a common response to avoid with an MI-consistent one. Use them as models for your own words, close to what the employee said.

If you want the theory behind OARS first, read [OARS in motivational interviewing](/guides/oars-motivational-interviewing). This page is the workplace phrasebook.

## Open questions

Open questions invite the employee to tell you what matters, in their own words. At work, the most common mistake is a closed question that guesses the answer.

### Starting a check-in

<Example speaker="Sterile processing tech">It's fine. Busy.</Example>
<Example speaker="SPD supervisor" kind="avoid">Is it the new tray counts?</Example>
<Example speaker="SPD supervisor" kind="open-question">What's been taking up most of your shift lately?</Example>

The closed version picks the topic for him. The open one lets him choose.

### Asking about a problem

<Example speaker="Scheduler">I know the template's been a mess.</Example>
<Example speaker="Practice manager" kind="avoid">Why didn't you flag it sooner?</Example>
<Example speaker="Practice manager" kind="open-question">What's been making the template hard to keep up?</Example>

"Why didn't you" asks for a defense. "What's been making it hard" asks for information.

### Asking about their goals

<Example speaker="Medical assistant">I don't want to be rooming patients forever.</Example>
<Example speaker="Clinic supervisor" kind="avoid">Have you thought about the LPN program?</Example>
<Example speaker="Clinic supervisor" kind="open-question">Where would you want to be in a couple of years?</Example>

### Asking about a change

<Example speaker="Pharmacist">The new dispensing cabinets are a step backward.</Example>
<Example speaker="Pharmacy director" kind="avoid">Don't you think they'll be safer?</Example>
<Example speaker="Pharmacy director" kind="open-question">What are you seeing with them that worries you?</Example>

A question that starts with "Don't you think" argues while pretending to ask.

### Asking about next steps

<Example speaker="Billing specialist">I want to get my denials under fifty again.</Example>
<Example speaker="Revenue cycle manager" kind="open-question">What would be a first step this week?</Example>

### Asking about a safety concern

<Example speaker="Surgical tech">The new count sheet is confusing. Somebody's going to miss a sponge.</Example>
<Example speaker="OR manager" kind="avoid">Are you saying you haven't been doing the counts right?</Example>
<Example speaker="OR manager" kind="open-question">Which part of the sheet are people getting tripped up on?</Example>

The open version treats the tech as someone reporting a hazard, which is what he's doing.

## Affirmations

An affirmation names a specific effort or value. Managers often default to praise ("Great job!"), which is pleasant and forgettable. A specific affirmation tells the employee you saw what they did.

### After a hard moment

<Example speaker="Front desk lead">That man screamed at me for ten minutes about his copay.</Example>
<Example speaker="Practice manager" kind="avoid">You did great! Don't let it get to you.</Example>
<Example speaker="Practice manager" kind="affirmation">You kept your voice level and kept explaining his options the whole time. That took patience.</Example>

### When they're doubting themselves

<Example speaker="New unit clerk">I'm so slow compared to Denise.</Example>
<Example speaker="Nurse manager" kind="avoid">You'll get there. Denise has been here fifteen years.</Example>
<Example speaker="Nurse manager" kind="affirmation">You double-check every order before you enter it. That's why your entries are clean.</Example>

### Affirming effort, even without results

<Example speaker="Analyst">I got the reports in on the fifth. Still late, I know.</Example>
<Example speaker="Team lead" kind="affirmation">You cut three days off last month. You've been working at this.</Example>

You can affirm the effort and still hold the standard. The deadline is still the third.

### Affirming a team

<Example speaker="Unit manager" kind="avoid">Great job last night, everyone!</Example>
<Example speaker="Unit manager" kind="affirmation">Three admissions in the last two hours, and every one of them had a med rec done before shift change. You covered for each other.</Example>

Team affirmations work the same way. Name what the team did, so they know what you noticed.

### Affirming a value

<Example speaker="Respiratory therapist">I stayed to finish teaching the family how to use the nebulizer.</Example>
<Example speaker="RT supervisor" kind="affirmation">You want families to leave knowing what they're doing.</Example>

Affirming a value connects the behavior to who they are, and people tend to repeat what they hear named that way.

## Reflections

Reflections are the core MI skill. You say back what you heard, often with a guess at the meaning underneath. They tell the employee you're listening and invite them to say more. At work, the move to avoid is usually a quick fix or a defense.

### Simple reflection

A simple reflection stays close to their words.

<Example speaker="Lab tech">I've asked for a second centrifuge three times.</Example>
<Example speaker="Lab manager" kind="avoid">Budget's tight this year. I'll see what I can do.</Example>
<Example speaker="Lab manager" kind="reflection">You've asked three times and nothing's happened.</Example>

### Complex reflection

A complex reflection adds meaning, often the feeling or value under the words.

<Example speaker="Imaging tech">I'm the only one who knows how to run the old CT, so I get pulled every time.</Example>
<Example speaker="Radiology manager" kind="avoid">That's because you're the best at it!</Example>
<Example speaker="Radiology manager" kind="complex-reflection">Being the expert has turned into being on call for everyone.</Example>

### Reflecting a feeling

<Example speaker="Patient access rep">I found out about the new schedule from a group text.</Example>
<Example speaker="Patient access manager" kind="avoid">That was a mistake on our end. It won't happen again.</Example>
<Example speaker="Patient access manager" kind="complex-reflection">You felt left out of a decision that changes your week.</Example>

The apology may be right, and you can offer it next. Reflect first so they know you understood what stung.

### Double-sided reflection

When someone voices both sides, a double-sided reflection holds them together, with their reason for change last.

<Example speaker="Physical therapist">Documenting in the room slows my sessions down. But I hate staying an hour late to chart.</Example>
<Example speaker="Rehab manager" kind="complex-reflection">Charting in the room costs you time with patients, and charting after costs you your evenings.</Example>

### Reflecting discord

When the tension is with you, reflect that first.

<Example speaker="Medical assistant">Why does it feel like you're always watching me?</Example>
<Example speaker="Clinic supervisor" kind="avoid">I'm not watching you. I check everyone's numbers.</Example>
<Example speaker="Clinic supervisor" kind="complex-reflection">It feels like I've been singling you out.</Example>

The defense argues with her experience. The reflection accepts it as her experience, which lets her tell you what's behind it.

### Reflecting change talk

When you hear the employee's own reason for change, reflect that part and let the rest go by.

<Example speaker="Care coordinator">It's not like I'm ignoring the callbacks. Although Mrs. Abubakar waiting a week wasn't okay.</Example>
<Example speaker="Clinic manager" kind="reflection">You don't want patients waiting that long.</Example>

## Summaries

A summary gathers what you've heard and hands it back so the employee can correct it or build on it. Managers use summaries to close a conversation or change topics. They also help when an employee is spiraling and you need to slow down.

### Closing a one-on-one

<Example speaker="Clinic supervisor" kind="summary">So the mornings are where it falls apart, mostly because two rooms aren't stocked. You'd like to come in feeling ready, and you think a ten-minute restock at close might do it. Did I miss anything?</Example>

### Transitioning to planning

<Example speaker="Revenue cycle manager" kind="summary">You don't love the new denial workflow, and you think it adds steps. You also said the old way meant you were finding problems three weeks late. Where does that leave you?</Example>

### Slowing things down

<Example speaker="Department lead" kind="summary">Let me make sure I have it. The new hire is struggling, you're covering her work, and you've stopped taking lunch. That's a lot at once. Which piece do you want to start with?</Example>

## Two moves that sit alongside OARS

### Asking permission

Ask before you give advice or raise a topic, even when it's required.

<Example speaker="Clinic manager">Would it be all right if I shared what's worked at the other clinic?</Example>

### Stating an expectation

MI at work includes being direct about what isn't negotiable. Say it once, then hand back the how.

<Example speaker="Nursing director" kind="avoid">It'd be great if folks could maybe try to round a little more on nights.</Example>
<Example speaker="Nursing director">Hourly rounding is required on this unit, including nights. What would help you keep up with it after midnight?</Example>
