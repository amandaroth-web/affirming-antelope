The best way to practice motivational interviewing is in short, focused sessions that target one skill and give you feedback on each attempt. Good exercises include writing reflections at several depths, peer role-plays with an observer, and recording yourself, then counting your questions and reflections. AI role-play adds volume between sessions, and feedback from a skilled trainer is what keeps you improving.

## Why reading about MI isn't enough

MI is easy to understand and hard to do. Most people leave a workshop able to define a complex reflection. Far fewer can produce one when a patient says "I don't need that medication" or an employee says "You don't know what it's like out here."

Research on MI training suggests that a workshop alone rarely produces lasting skill, and that feedback and coaching afterward make a real difference. Knowing about the righting reflex doesn't stop it. Practice does.

## Principles of deliberate practice

The practice that builds skill fastest has a few features.

- **One skill at a time.** "Practice MI" is too broad. "Write three complex reflections for this statement" is specific enough to improve.
- **At the edge of your ability.** If it feels easy, move to a harder version. If you're failing every attempt, step back.
- **Fast feedback.** You need to know whether each attempt worked, ideally within seconds or minutes.
- **Repetition.** Ten reflections on ten statements will teach you more than one long conversation you never review.
- **Transfer.** Bring what you practice into real conversations, then notice how it went.

## Exercises you can do alone

### Five reflections for one statement

Take a single statement and write five different reflections: a repeat, a rephrase, a paraphrase, a reflection of feeling and a double-sided reflection.

<Example speaker="Patient">I know I should take it every day. I just hate how it makes me feel in the mornings.</Example>

- Repeat: "You hate how it makes you feel in the mornings."
- Rephrase: "The mornings are rough on it."
- Paraphrase: "You're trading a bad morning for something you know is good for you."
- Feeling: "It's frustrating to feel worse from the thing that's supposed to help."
- Double-sided: "The mornings are hard on it, and you also know it matters to take it every day."

The [reflective listening guide](/guides/reflective-listening-examples) explains each level.

### Closed-to-open rewrites

List ten closed questions you ask often and write an open version of each. Read both out loud. The [open-ended questions guide](/guides/open-ended-questions-examples) has examples.

### Change talk hunting

Listen to a podcast interview, a meeting or a TV drama and tally change talk and sustain talk. Mark which type of DARN-CAT you hear. This trains your ear without the pressure of responding. See [change talk vs sustain talk](/guides/change-talk-vs-sustain-talk) for the categories.

### Short drills

Answer one prompt at a time, then compare your response to a model and an explanation. The app's drills work this way.

## Practicing with a peer

### Real-plays over role-plays

In a **role-play**, one person acts as a scripted client. In a **real-play**, the speaker talks about a real but low-stakes change in their own life, such as exercising more, cleaning out the garage or answering email faster. Real-plays produce more realistic ambivalence because the feelings are real. Keep topics light, since this is practice and not counseling.

### Triads with an observer

Work in groups of three: speaker, listener and observer. Run a 7 to 10 minute conversation. The observer keeps a simple tally sheet:

- Open questions
- Closed questions
- Simple reflections
- Complex reflections
- Affirmations
- Advice or persuasion without permission

Then debrief in this order: the speaker says what felt helpful, the listener says what they noticed, and the observer shares the counts. Rotate roles.

### Tag-team reflections

In a group, one person reads a statement and each member offers a reflection in turn, each a little deeper than the last. It's fast, low-stakes and shows how many ways there are to reflect one sentence.

## Recording and self-coding

Listening back to your own conversations is one of the most useful habits you can build. Record a role-play or real-play (with everyone's permission) and code it yourself.

Only record real patient or employee conversations with their consent and within your organization's privacy rules. Role-plays and real-plays are usually the better place to start.

### What to count

The **MITI** (Motivational Interviewing Treatment Integrity code) is a widely used system for rating MI conversations. A trained coder rates the whole conversation on four global dimensions: cultivating change talk, softening sustain talk, partnership and empathy. They also count specific behaviors, including:

- Questions
- Simple and complex reflections
- Affirmations
- Seeking collaboration and emphasizing autonomy
- Giving information
- Persuading, with or without permission
- Confronting

You don't need formal training to borrow the ideas. Tally your questions, reflections and MI-inconsistent moves, like unasked-for advice or arguing.

### Two numbers to watch

- **Reflection-to-question ratio.** Divide your reflections by your questions. The MITI manual treats 1:1 as fair and 2:1 as good.
- **Percent complex reflections.** Divide complex reflections by all reflections. Around 40% is fair and 50% is good.

Don't chase the numbers at the expense of the conversation. A high ratio with shallow reflections isn't better than a lower one with great ones. Use the counts to spot habits, like asking three questions in a row or slipping into advice.

### Questions to ask yourself

- Where did I hear change talk, and how did I respond?
- Where did I feel the righting reflex, and what did I do with it?
- What's one moment I'd do differently?

## Common practice mistakes

- **Practicing only what you're good at.** If your reflections are solid, spend your time on summaries or on responding to discord.
- **Running long role-plays with no review.** A 20-minute role-play you never discuss teaches less than three 5-minute ones with feedback after each.
- **Making role-play clients too easy.** Clients who agree with everything let you skip the hard parts. Ask your partner to push back and to hold their ambivalence.
- **Making them impossible.** A partner who resists every move teaches you to brace for a fight. Aim for realistic ambivalence instead.
- **Skipping the debrief.** The learning happens when you look at what you did and why. Build in time for it.

## Where AI role-play helps, and where it doesn't

AI role-play partners, including the ones in this app, are useful for some parts of practice and limited for others.

**Where it helps:**

- **Volume.** You can practice at 10 p.m. without coordinating with a peer.
- **Low stakes.** Nobody is watching, so you can try a risky reflection and see what happens.
- **Range.** You can practice with a defensive charge nurse, a skeptical patient or a quiet new hire in the same week.
- **Quick feedback.** You can get an immediate read on whether a response was a question, a reflection or advice.

**Where it falls short:**

- **It isn't a real person.** AI characters can be too cooperative, too consistent or oddly articulate. Real ambivalence is messier.
- **It can be wrong.** AI feedback can mislabel a response or praise a weak one. Treat it as a second opinion, not a verdict.
- **It misses voice and body.** Tone, pacing, silence and facial expression matter in MI, and text practice doesn't capture them.
- **It can't judge context.** Whether a reflection fits depends on the relationship, the setting and what came before. A trainer can weigh that. An AI often can't.

Think of the AI as a practice partner and not the authority. Use it to get reps in between sessions with a trainer, coach or peer group, and bring your questions and recordings to people who can give you skilled feedback. A trainer listening to a coded recording will catch things no drill will.

## A simple weekly plan

- **Daily (5 minutes):** one or two short drills.
- **Twice a week (15 minutes):** a role-play, with AI or a peer, focused on one skill.
- **Weekly:** listen back to one recorded practice conversation and tally your questions and reflections.
- **Monthly:** get feedback from a trainer or experienced MI practitioner on a recording.

Adjust to your schedule. Consistency matters more than session length. If you run MI training, [our trainer page](/for/trainers) shows how to assign drills and review learners' practice between sessions.
