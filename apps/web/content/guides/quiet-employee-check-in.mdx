To check in with a quiet or disengaged employee, start by saying why you're meeting and that it isn't about a mistake. Ask one open question, give them time to answer, and reflect what you hear before you offer anything. If they don't open up the first time, leave the door open and try again. The goal of a first check-in is to understand, and solving can wait.

## Lower the stakes in the first minute

You hold power over this person's job. When a manager asks to talk, many employees assume they're in trouble, and a quiet employee may already be braced for it. Clear that up first.

<Example speaker="Medical assistant">You wanted to see me? Did I do something wrong?</Example>
<Example speaker="Clinic supervisor" kind="avoid">No, no, nothing like that. So, how are things?</Example>
<Example speaker="Clinic supervisor">Nothing's wrong, and this isn't about your work, which has been solid. I've noticed you've been quieter in huddles lately, and I wanted to check in on how you're doing.</Example>

The quick "no, no" leaves her guessing why she's there. The better version says what this is about and what it isn't, and it names one specific observation without a judgment attached. Engaging, the first process of MI, depends on the employee feeling safe enough to talk.

## Describe what you've noticed, without a label

"You seem checked out" and "You're not as engaged as you used to be" are conclusions. The employee has to defend against them. A specific observation gives them something to respond to.

<Example speaker="Pharmacy manager" kind="avoid">You've seemed pretty disengaged lately. Is everything okay?</Example>
<Example speaker="Pharmacy manager">You used to be the first one to flag problems with the new system, and I haven't heard from you in a few weeks. I wanted to ask how it's going.</Example>

The second version includes a small affirmation, too. She used to flag problems, and you valued it. That tells her you noticed her contribution as well as her silence.

## Ask one open question and wait

Quiet employees often answer in a few words at first. The common response is to fill the gap with more questions, usually closed ones that guess at the problem. That turns a check-in into an interrogation.

<Example speaker="Patient access rep">I'm fine. Just been busy.</Example>
<Example speaker="Patient access manager" kind="avoid">Is it the new registration rules? Or is something going on at home? Are you getting along with the team?</Example>
<Example speaker="Patient access manager" kind="open-question">Busy how?</Example>

Then wait. Five seconds of silence feels long to you and gives them time to decide whether to say more. If the answer is still short, a reflection often works better than another question.

## Reflect what's under the short answers

A tentative reflection that names a possible feeling can help a guarded employee feel understood. Hold it lightly. If you're wrong, they'll correct you, and the correction tells you something.

<Example speaker="Unit secretary">It's whatever. Same as always.</Example>
<Example speaker="Nurse manager" kind="complex-reflection">Sounds like work has started to feel like something to get through.</Example>
<Example speaker="Unit secretary">Kind of. I don't know. It used to feel like people noticed what I did.</Example>

That last sentence is worth slowing down for. Reflect it before you respond to it.

<Example speaker="Nurse manager" kind="reflection">You've stopped feeling noticed.</Example>
<Example speaker="Unit secretary">Since the reorg, yeah. Nobody asks me anything anymore. They just send it to the new coordinator.</Example>

You now know more than any survey would have told you: a specific change, and the effect it had on her.

## Hold off on fixing

Once an employee opens up, the urge to solve the problem is strong. Resist it for a little longer. Offering solutions too early tells them the conversation is over, and the first thing they mention is often not the main thing.

<Example speaker="Medical assistant">My dad's been in and out of the hospital since August. It's been a lot.</Example>
<Example speaker="Clinic supervisor" kind="avoid">Oh no. Have you looked into FMLA? And the EAP has a caregiver program, I can send you the link.</Example>
<Example speaker="Clinic supervisor" kind="reflection">You've been carrying your dad's health on top of everything here.</Example>
<Example speaker="Medical assistant">Yeah. I drive four hours to see him every weekend, so I'm wiped by Monday. I didn't want anyone to think I can't handle it.</Example>

The reflection got you two more pieces of information: why she's exhausted on Mondays, and the worry about how it looks. When the time comes to talk about options, you'll be solving the right problem. Then offer resources with permission: "Would it help if I told you about a couple of options we have for caregivers?"

## If they don't open up

Some people won't share much the first time, and that's their right. Respect it, and leave the door open.

<Example speaker="Respiratory therapist">I'm okay, promise. It's nothing.</Example>
<Example speaker="RT supervisor">Okay. I'm glad you're okay. If anything changes, or if there's something I could do differently, my door's open. I'll check in again in a couple of weeks either way.</Example>

The promise to check in again matters. It shows the first conversation wasn't a one-time box to tick. Many employees open up at the second or third check-in, once they've seen nothing bad happened after the first.

## Quiet isn't always a problem

Some employees are quiet by temperament and have always been. The signal worth checking on is a change: a talkative person who's gone silent, or a reliable person who's started leaving the minute the shift ends. If someone has always been quiet, ask how they like to contribute. Some people prefer to raise ideas one-on-one or in writing.

<Example speaker="Lab manager" kind="open-question">I'd like to make sure your ideas make it into the huddle. What's the easiest way for you to share them?</Example>

## Check-in questions to try

A few open questions work well with a quiet employee. Use one, then listen.

- "How have things been for you lately, at work or otherwise?"
- "What's been the hardest part of the last few weeks?"
- "What part of the job still feels good?"
- "Is there anything about how the team works that's been getting to you?"
- "What would make work a little easier right now?"

Avoid "Is everything okay?" It invites "Yes," and the conversation ends there. Avoid "What's wrong?" too. It assumes something is, and asks them to name a problem before they trust you with it.

For more ways to turn a closed question into an open one, see [open-ended questions in MI](/guides/open-ended-questions-examples).

## Following up on team issues

Quiet employees sometimes withdraw because of friction with a coworker. If that comes up, reflect it, thank them for telling you, and ask what they'd want to happen next. Don't promise a specific outcome before you know more.

## The limits of MI in a check-in

- **Performance problems.** If the employee's work has slipped, that's a separate conversation. Don't fold it into a wellbeing check-in, or the next check-in will feel like a trap.
- **Harassment, safety or risk of harm.** If they disclose harassment, a safety issue or thoughts of harming themselves, you may have reporting obligations. Don't promise confidentiality you can't keep. Tell them what you'll need to do, and connect them to support.
- **When they need more than a manager.** Grief and mental health conditions call for professional support. MI helps you listen well, and the EAP or a counselor can take it from there.
