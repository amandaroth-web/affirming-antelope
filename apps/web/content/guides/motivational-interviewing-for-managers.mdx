Motivational interviewing helps managers have conversations about change that don't turn into lectures. You stay clear about what's required, and you draw out the employee's own reasons and ideas for how to get there. It fits missed deadlines, performance concerns, burnout and resistance to change. It doesn't fit emergencies, or decisions that are yours to make.

## Why MI fits management

Most management problems eventually become conversations about change. Someone is missing deadlines, cutting corners or pulling away. You can tell them what to do, and sometimes that's right. But if the change depends on their effort and judgment, telling rarely gets you more than compliance, and often not even that.

MI starts from a different assumption: the person already has some reasons to change, and your job is to help them hear those reasons. A front desk lead who hates the new check-in process may also say the paper forms are a mess. That second sentence is the one to work with.

The research base for MI comes mostly from healthcare. Its use in management is an adaptation, and the workplace evidence is much thinner. The underlying skills, like listening well, asking rather than telling, and respecting autonomy, are ones most leaders already know matter.

## What's different when you're the boss

Two things make MI harder for managers than for a clinician or coach.

**You hold power.** A request to talk can sound like a warning. Be honest about why you're meeting, early. "This isn't about a mistake. I've noticed you seem quieter and wanted to check in."

**You have a stake.** When the outcome affects your team's numbers, the urge to fix and persuade gets stronger. MI calls this the **righting reflex**. The more the result matters to you, the more you'll need to notice it and reflect before you advise.

Neither of these rules MI out. They mean you need to be transparent about your role and clear about which parts of the situation are open.

## Opening the conversation

How you start sets the tone for everything after. Three moves help.

**Say why you're meeting.** Ambiguity makes people defensive. "I'd like to talk about the month-end reports. I'm not here to write anyone up. I want to understand what's getting in the way."

**Ask before you tell.** Before you share your view, find out theirs. "How has month-end been going from your side?" You'll often learn something that changes what you were going to say.

**Ask permission to raise your topic.** When a check-in has drifted, a permission question turns the corner without a jolt.

<Example speaker="Medical assistant">Anyway, my daughter's soccer team made the finals, so that's been our whole weekend.</Example>
<Example speaker="Clinic manager" kind="avoid">Okay, enough about the weekend. We need to talk about your chart notes.</Example>
<Example speaker="Clinic manager">That's a big weekend for your family. Would it be okay if we spent a few minutes on the chart notes before we wrap up?</Example>

Permission questions work even when the topic isn't optional, because they give the person a moment to get ready.

## Separate the what from the how

The most useful tool for managers is separating what's required from how it gets done. Name the non-negotiables plainly. Then hand the rest back.

<Example speaker="Analyst">So what exactly do you need from me?</Example>
<Example speaker="Revenue cycle manager" kind="avoid">Well, ideally I'd love it if you could maybe try to get things in a bit earlier?</Example>
<Example speaker="Revenue cycle manager">I need the month-end reports by the third business day. How you get there is up to you. What would make that doable?</Example>

The vague version sounds kind and leaves the analyst guessing. The clear version is direct about the deadline and still treats the analyst as the expert on their own workflow.

The same goes for safety and compliance.

<Example speaker="Medical assistant">Do I really have to do the full wipe-down between every patient? It's so slow.</Example>
<Example speaker="Clinic director" kind="avoid">It's policy. I don't make the rules.</Example>
<Example speaker="Clinic director">The full wipe-down is required, and I hear that it slows you down. What would make it easier to fit into your flow?</Example>

"I don't make the rules" is clear but distances you and ends the conversation. Pretending it's optional would be worse. The MI-consistent response is honest about the requirement and curious about the barrier.

## Giving information and advice with permission

MI doesn't mean you never give advice. It means you give it in a way that leaves room for the person's judgment. The pattern is often called **ask, offer, ask** (or elicit, provide, elicit).

1. **Ask** what they know or what they've tried. "What have you heard about how the float pool rotation works?"
2. **Offer** the information or suggestion, briefly and without pressure. "It goes by hours worked this quarter, not seniority."
3. **Ask** what they make of it. "How does that fit with what you expected?"

When someone asks you to tell them what to do, respect that.

<Example speaker="Scheduler">Can you just tell me what you want me to do? I don't need a whole conversation about it.</Example>
<Example speaker="Department lead">Fair. I do have a couple of ideas. Want to hear them, and you tell me which ones would work?</Example>

## Four common situations

### Missed deadlines

Start with curiosity, not the deadline. The reasons for repeated lateness are usually more specific than "doesn't care."

<Example speaker="Team lead" kind="open-question">What's been happening around month-end lately?</Example>
<Example speaker="Analyst">The data doesn't come in from billing until the second. I can't start until then.</Example>
<Example speaker="Team lead" kind="reflection">You're starting the month already behind.</Example>
<Example speaker="Analyst">Yeah. And I know the team can't close without my reports. I don't love being the bottleneck.</Example>

That last line is change talk: the analyst's own reason to get the reports in. Reflect it ("Being someone the team can count on matters to you") and ask what they'd want to try. You may also learn about a process problem you can fix.

### Performance conversations

Be specific about what you've observed, then ask for their view before you share your conclusions.

<Example speaker="Clinic manager">I've noticed three of the last five chart notes were done after the patient left. I'd like to hear how it looks from your side.</Example>

Then listen. Reflect what you hear. Use a summary before you move to next steps: "So the afternoons are where it falls apart, and you'd like to leave with notes done. What would you want to try?"

### Burnout and overload

Burnout conversations call for engaging first and fixing later. Reflect the load and the feeling.

<Example speaker="Charge nurse">I keep picking up extra shifts because nobody else will. I don't mind, really. It's just that my kids are starting to notice.</Example>
<Example speaker="Nurse manager" kind="complex-reflection">You care about the unit, and you're starting to feel what it's costing your family.</Example>

Two cautions. MI is a conversation style and not a treatment for burnout. If someone seems seriously distressed, point them toward your employee assistance program or other support. And some burnout comes from staffing and workload you control. Listening well doesn't replace fixing what's yours to fix.

### Resistance to change

When a new system or policy lands badly, you'll hear a mix of sustain talk and discord. Resist defending the decision.

<Example speaker="Veteran nurse">This is another decision made by people in suits who've never touched a patient. And now you want me to sell it to my team?</Example>
<Example speaker="Director" kind="avoid">I understand, but leadership looked at a lot of data before deciding this.</Example>
<Example speaker="Director" kind="complex-reflection">You weren't asked, and now you're being put in the middle with your team. How you talk with them about it is your call.</Example>

The defensive reply argues for the decision, which invites more argument against it. The reflection names the frustration and respects the nurse's autonomy without pretending the decision is open. For more on this, see [how to respond to sustain talk and discord](/guides/responding-to-sustain-talk).

## When MI isn't the right approach

- **Emergencies and safety.** If someone is about to do something dangerous, direct them.
- **Decisions that are yours.** If you've decided, say so. "I've decided we're moving to the new schedule in March" is more respectful than pretending to ask.
- **Formal discipline.** HR processes have rules. Use MI's listening skills to keep the meeting respectful, but don't imply choices the process doesn't allow.
- **When the person is ready.** If they've decided to change and want help planning, help them plan.
- **When you'd be manipulating.** If you're using open questions to lead someone to a conclusion you've already picked, stop and say what you think.

## Building the habit

Managers usually know what good listening looks like. The hard part is doing it when you're under pressure and the outcome matters. Practice the short moments: reflecting before you respond to pushback, stating an expectation without hedging, asking permission before advice.
